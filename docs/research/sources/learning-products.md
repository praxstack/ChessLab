# My verdict

For **seriously learning chess—not merely maintaining a streak—the best single paid option is Chess.com Platinum**.

For the **best value**, start free with:

**Duolingo Chess for the basics → Lichess for playing and analysis → Chessbrah’s Building Habits → Daniel Naroditsky’s speedruns → Take Take Take for plain-English game reviews.**

That free combination is stronger than relying only on Duolingo, Magnus Trainer, or random YouTube videos.

My overall ranking is:

1. **Chess.com Platinum:** best complete paid system.
2. **Lichess + carefully selected video series + Take Take Take:** best free system.
3. **Chessly:** best structured, entertaining video-first course.
4. **Duolingo Chess:** best absolute-beginner onboarding and habit formation.
5. **Take Take Take:** excellent new free game-review supplement, but not yet a complete curriculum.
6. **ChessMood:** deepest premium curriculum reviewed, but far too expensive for most learners in India.
7. **Magnus Trainer / Play Magnus:** charming, but not sensible as your main learning system in 2026.

## First: “mastering chess” needs clarification

Becoming good at online chess is realistic with disciplined self-study. Becoming an official chess master is a much larger project. Current FIDE title thresholds include:

* Candidate Master: **2200 FIDE**
* FIDE Master: **2300 FIDE**
* International Master: **2400 FIDE plus norms**
* Grandmaster: **2500 FIDE plus norms**

Online ratings and FIDE ratings are not interchangeable. No app or video course by itself takes somebody from beginner to FIDE Master. ([handbook.fide.com][1])

# What actually causes improvement

A new 2026 longitudinal study followed **44,213 Chess.com players** for six months. Players spent over 90% of their platform time simply playing, but deliberate-practice activities—lessons, reviews and puzzles together—were associated with **3.61 times more rating improvement per hour** than gameplay. ([Sage Journals][2])

When separated:

* Game reviews were associated with **5.58×** the improvement per hour of gameplay.
* Video lessons were associated with **6.60×**.
* Random puzzle volume was not significantly more efficient than gameplay in that analysis.

This was observational rather than a randomized experiment, so these are associations, not guaranteed causal effects. But the practical lesson is strong: **playing hundreds of games is not enough; reviewing mistakes and studying explanations matter enormously.** ([Sage Journals][2])

Chess intuition is not mysterious instinct. It is mostly:

* Recognizing familiar tactical and positional patterns.
* Knowing which candidate moves deserve calculation.
* Remembering plans from analogous positions.
* Repeatedly seeing why certain moves succeed or fail.

Spacing and actively retrieving previously learned ideas improve long-term retention more than repeatedly consuming the same explanation. That is why interactive drills, paused videos, repeated positions and self-testing beat passive watching. ([Sage Journals][2])

## 1. Chess.com: best overall paid system

Chess.com combines all four things a learner needs:

* Real opponents.
* A step-by-step lesson curriculum.
* Tactical and endgame training.
* Review of the games you personally played.

Its current lesson system covers openings, strategy, tactics and other subjects, while Game Review lets you revisit mistakes and retry important positions. ([Chess.com][3])

### Why Platinum is the correct plan

**Gold** gives unlimited lessons, videos, puzzles, opening explorer, endgames and other training tools—but not unlimited Game Review.

**Platinum** adds unlimited Game Review and analysis.

**Diamond** adds coach explanations for every move, Insights and more advanced statistics. ([Chess.com Help Center][4])

Therefore:

* Gold is adequate for lessons and tactics.
* **Platinum is the improvement sweet spot.**
* Diamond is more comfortable, but usually not necessary.

### Strengths

Chess.com is the best option for turning the learning cycle into:

> Play → identify mistakes → understand the reason → retry the position → study the missing concept → play again.

That feedback loop is much more useful than completing isolated lessons without seeing how they connect to your actual games.

### Weaknesses

Chess.com can also become a giant distraction machine. A player may spend an hour on bullet chess, Puzzle Rush, bots and achievements while doing almost no deliberate study.

Game Review can also become passive: clicking “show best move” is not analysis. The correct order is:

1. Review the game yourself.
2. Mark where you felt uncertain.
3. Calculate alternatives.
4. Only then open Game Review or the engine.

Used properly, Chess.com is excellent. Used as a blitz arcade, its learning value drops dramatically.

## 2. Duolingo Chess: best onboarding, not the best road to mastery

Duolingo’s current course begins with how pieces move, then gradually shifts from guided moves to open-ended problems. Around **75% of the course is puzzle-based**, with the remainder consisting of mini-games and full games. Duolingo says the course can help learners work toward 1500 Elo, but that is a platform claim—not an independently validated outcome or a FIDE-equivalent rating. ([Duolingo Blog][5])

### Where Duolingo is genuinely excellent

It is exceptionally good at:

* Making a complete beginner open the app daily.
* Removing fear and confusion around the board.
* Teaching piece movement and basic rules.
* Introducing elementary tactical patterns.
* Providing a clear next lesson instead of an overwhelming library.
* Making learning feel light enough to continue.

### Where it becomes insufficient

The same bite-sized design that builds a habit is weaker for:

* Calculating a variation for five or ten minutes.
* Comparing several candidate moves.
* Analysing an entire lost game.
* Building a coherent opening repertoire.
* Understanding long-term pawn structures.
* Training deep concentration.
* Studying complex endgames.

Duolingo is therefore a **launchpad**. It should not become your permanent chess universe.

A reasonable transition is:

* Completely new: use it for two to six weeks.
* Once you comfortably understand legal moves, checks, captures, basic mates and simple tactics: move most of your time to real games, analysis and structured lessons.

## 3. “Magnus chess course” can mean four different products

### A. Magnus Trainer

The official Magnus Trainer listing describes over **250 lessons**, mini-games and thousands of puzzles covering foundations, openings and endgames. However, the current official listing is iPhone-oriented, and its last recorded iOS update was **April 30, 2021**. ([App Store][6])

It can still be fun for:

* Early pattern recognition.
* Board visualization.
* Tactical mini-games.
* Learners motivated by attractive design.

But I would not build a 2026 learning plan around an app that has gone roughly five years without a listed update.

**Do not purchase its ₹29,900 lifetime option.**

Also, an Android app called **Magnus Chess Academy** is published by Flico Technologies and only says it is “inspired by” Magnus Carlsen. It is not the official Magnus/Chess.com product. ([Google Play][7])

### B. Play Magnus

Play Magnus lets you play simulated versions of Magnus and other players at different ages. It has hints, takebacks and supporting content, but it is mainly:

* Entertainment.
* Motivation.
* A novelty challenge.
* A way to test yourself against increasingly strong bots.

It is not a full beginner-to-advanced curriculum. Its Indian App Store listing shows a ₹159 monthly subscription and various individual add-ons. ([App Store][8])

### C. Take Take Take

This is the most interesting Magnus-linked option in 2026.

Take Take Take is co-founded by Magnus Carlsen and currently positions itself for approximately **800–2000 Elo** players. After a game, its digital coach explains mistakes in ordinary language rather than only showing engine scores and variations. It is connected to Lichess infrastructure for online play. ([Google Play][9])

The platform launched its current play-and-improvement experience in April 2026, with the social feed, Play Zone and current Game Review features offered free. Its legal terms nevertheless describe a freemium model in which some features require—or may later require—a subscription. ([Take Take Take][10])

This is a promising **free Game Review alternative**, particularly on Android. Its limitation is that it does not yet provide the same complete, sequenced curriculum as Chess.com, Chessly or ChessMood.

### D. The Magnus Touch on Chessable

Magnus has specialist Chessable courses such as:

* *The Magnus Touch: Chess Strategy*
* *The Magnus Touch: Endgame*

Each is currently listed at **$39.99 without video** or **$99.98 with video**. These are focused courses, not beginner-to-master programmes. ([chessable.com][11])

They are more suitable once you are approximately 1200+ online and already understand basic tactics, development, king safety and elementary endings. Buying a Magnus strategy course before those foundations would be inefficient.

Chessable itself is excellent for remembering positions through spaced repetition, but its paid course prices and separate Pro membership can become expensive. Its current India App Store lists Chessable Pro at ₹1,049 monthly or ₹6,400 annually. ([Google Play][12])

## 4. Video courses: useful only when made interactive

Watching a grandmaster explain a game feels educational, but it can produce an illusion of understanding. The instructor finds the moves; you merely recognize them after they are shown.

For a video to train chess intuition:

1. Pause before every critical move.
2. Identify checks, captures and threats.
3. Write two or three candidate moves.
4. Calculate each candidate.
5. Commit to one.
6. Resume the explanation.
7. Save the position if your thinking was wrong.

### Chessly

Chessly, built around GothamChess/Levy Rozman, currently includes over **70 opening and skills courses**, videos, drills, quizzes, XP and achievements. It is intentionally designed for newer and improving players. ([Chessly][13])

Its biggest strengths are:

* Clear, conversational explanations.
* Good emphasis on practical plans.
* Strong motivation and presentation.
* Less dryness than traditional chess courses.
* Better structure than browsing random YouTube videos.

Its weaknesses:

* It does not replace a full playing and analysis platform.
* It can lead to consuming too many openings.
* It is expensive compared with India-localized Chess.com pricing.
* Its usefulness depends on whether you enjoy Levy’s teaching style.

For a learner around 500–1600 who likes video teaching, it is probably the best paid video-first choice.

### ChessMood

ChessMood currently advertises more than **500 hours of grandmaster-created courses** and over **10,000 exercises**, with live events, recordings and community support depending on the plan. ([Chess Mood][14])

It is the most comprehensive curriculum among the products reviewed here and is especially strong for:

* Positional intuition.
* Structured opening repertoires.
* Tactical pattern systems.
* Endgames.
* Connecting strategic explanations with exercises.

Its price, however, is extremely difficult to justify for most Indian self-learners. A strong player who studies 10–15 hours every week may extract value from it. A casual learner almost certainly will not.

### Free video path

Two particularly useful free series are:

* **Chessbrah Building Habits:** best for absolute beginners and lower-rated players because it teaches simple repeatable rules rather than flashy tricks. ([YouTube][15])
* **Daniel Naroditsky’s Speedruns/Master Class/Sensei series:** better after basic familiarity, because he verbalizes calculation, positional evaluation and practical decision-making. ([YouTube][16])

These are better than generic “ten opening traps” videos because they show entire games and recurring thought processes.

# Best path according to your current level

## You do not yet know the rules or regularly hang pieces

Use:

* Duolingo Chess.
* Chessbrah Building Habits.
* Lichess or free Chess.com games.
* Optionally Dr. Wolf for interactive explanations.

Do not pay for an opening course. Do not memorize ten-move variations. Your priorities are legal moves, checkmates, undefended pieces, basic tactics and simple development.

## Approximately 500–1000 rapid

Best setup:

* Chess.com Platinum, or Lichess plus Take Take Take.
* Building Habits.
* Slow tactical exercises.
* Basic king-and-pawn and rook endgames.
* Analysis of every serious game.

At this level, removing one-move blunders is worth much more than studying fashionable openings.

## Approximately 1000–1500 rapid

Best setup:

* Chess.com Platinum for game review.
* Naroditsky speedruns or Chessly for structured explanation.
* A small opening repertoire based on plans, not memorization.
* Calculation training where you write the full line before moving.
* Regular endgame study.

This is the range where chess intuition begins to become noticeably richer because tactical patterns and positional ideas start connecting.

## Approximately 1500–2000 rapid

Apps become secondary. You need:

* Detailed self-analysis before engine analysis.
* Annotated master games.
* Targeted calculation work.
* Structured endgames.
* Opening positions arising in your actual games.
* A coach or stronger player periodically reviewing recurring weaknesses.

Take Take Take may be useful for explanation, while Chessable/Chessly specialist courses can address defined gaps. A generic beginner curriculum provides diminishing returns here.

## Above approximately 2000 rapid

Do not collect more beginner subscriptions. Use targeted materials, tournament games, databases, calculation books, serious opening preparation and coaching. Your personal mistakes are now more valuable than another broad “learn chess” course.

# The best routine for intuition and focus

A strong weekly system is more important than which logo is on the app.

For approximately **60–75 minutes a day, five days per week**:

### Two or three days: serious games

Play one **15+10 or 30-minute** game.

Immediately afterwards:

1. Analyse without an engine.
2. Mark three critical positions.
3. Write what you calculated and why you chose your move.
4. Then use Chess.com Game Review, Lichess analysis or Take Take Take.
5. Retry the critical positions.

Tighter time controls reduce the opportunity to use slower search and calculation processes, so bullet chess is poor primary training for focus. Keep bullet as entertainment rather than the centre of your programme. ([ResearchGate][25])

### Three days: calculation

Spend 15–20 minutes on a small number of difficult positions.

Do not guess moves rapidly. Before answering:

* Identify checks, captures and threats.
* Find candidate moves.
* Calculate the opponent’s strongest reply.
* State the final position you expect.

Five properly calculated puzzles are better than fifty instinctive swipes.

### Three days: structured learning

Spend 20–30 minutes on one coherent source:

* Chess.com curriculum, or
* Chessly, or
* Building Habits/Naroditsky, or
* A specific Chessable course.

Do not alternate between seven courses. Finish a theme, test it and use it in games.

### Once weekly: endgames

Start with:

* King and pawn against king.
* Opposition.
* Basic checkmates.
* Converting an extra pawn.
* Elementary rook endings.

Endgames improve calculation and piece coordination because there are fewer pieces but every move matters.

# Does chess improve general focus?

Chess certainly practises **chess-specific attention**: sitting with one position, suppressing impulsive moves, comparing alternatives and maintaining a calculation tree.

However, research on cognitive training finds that broad “far transfer”—for example, becoming generally more focused, intelligent or academically capable merely because one trained chess—is weak or null after stronger controls are applied. Therefore, do not buy a chess subscription on the promise that it will automatically fix concentration everywhere in life. ([Sage Journals][26])

For chess to train sustained attention rather than quick dopamine-seeking:

* Prefer 15+10 or longer.
* Disable notifications.
* Do not multitask with videos or social media.
* Physically write candidate moves during training.
* Finish one game and its analysis before starting another.
* Track whether you followed your thinking process—not merely whether you won.

# What I would purchase

My recommended sequence is:

**First 14 days: spend ₹0.**

Use Duolingo for the absolute basics, Lichess for games and analysis, Chessbrah Building Habits, and Take Take Take for additional game explanations.

After those two weeks, provided you are playing and reviewing games consistently:

**Buy Chess.com Platinum annual at approximately ₹2,499.**

It is dramatically better value in India than Chessly at roughly ₹8,564/year, Chessable Pro at ₹6,400/year before individual course purchases, or ChessMood at approximately ₹90,214/year and above.

Do **not** buy:

* Magnus Trainer lifetime.
* ChessMood at the beginning.
* Several Chessable opening courses.
* Diamond merely for statistics.
* Super Duolingo solely for chess.

Use **Chessly instead of Chess.com** only when you strongly prefer Levy’s teaching style and know that structured videos and drills will keep you studying. Use **The Magnus Touch** later as a specialist strategy/endgame resource—not as your first chess course.

So the clearest answer is:

> **Best single product: Chess.com Platinum.**
> **Best beginner habit-builder: Duolingo Chess.**
> **Best free serious system: Lichess + Building Habits + Naroditsky + Take Take Take.**
> **Best paid video course: Chessly.**
> **Best Magnus-linked product right now: Take Take Take, not the old Magnus Trainer.**
